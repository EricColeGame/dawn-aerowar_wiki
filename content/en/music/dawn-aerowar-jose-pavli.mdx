## Jose Pavli Is Scoring Dawn Aerowar — and the Soundtrack Is Nearly as Long as the Film Itself

Dawn Aerowar, the interactive animated film arriving on Steam November 10, 2026, carries 77 minutes of entirely original music composed by Jose Pavli — the same person who produced, directed, edited, and wrote the story. For a film with a runtime of nearly two hours across 12 episodes, that means the score was composed to picture, covering key moments rather than the whole runtime.

Pavli is no stranger to aviation-themed projects, and here he wears nearly every hat at once. The result is a score shaped by a single creative vision from storyboard to final mix.

## What the Score Actually Sounds Like

According to the [official Dawn Aerowar site](https://dawnaerowar.com/), the soundtrack is fast-paced and epic, built around a few distinctive elements:

- **Full choirs** lending the aerial combat a large-scale, cinematic weight
- **Bulgarian voices**, an unusual texture that gives the vocal music an otherworldly, urgent edge
- **Classical guitar**, grounding more intimate beats
- **Two original songs** woven into the film

That combination — Eastern European choral tradition meeting orchestral action scoring — is a deliberate choice for a story about one pilot, Zodiac 1, flying a last sortie across an empire that destroyed everything he loved.

## Why Composing to Picture Matters Here

Because the music was written against the finished animation rather than before it, cues can follow the choreography of each dogfight precisely. Every flight is staged and animated in 3D and "shot like a film," and a to-picture score is what makes aerial sequences land emotionally instead of feeling like a music video with planes.

The sound design, mixing, and mastering were handled by Anize Amestoy, working alongside the score so that radio chatter, engine noise, and music coexist cleanly.

## The Sound of the War: One Voice and a Score

The film's audio identity is built on contrast. Zodiac flies alone, and the war reaches him through the cockpit radio — an AWACS operator he's never met, sharp-tongued and strangely well informed. Through that single voice, the audience hears the whole conflict.

Against that intimacy, Pavli's score does the opposite work: choirs, orchestra, and epic pacing to convey the scale of the Saal'Runa Empire and the Free Nations' collapse. It's a classic dynamic — quiet cockpit, thunderous sky — and it's exactly where a to-picture composer earns their keep.
